    About this map

    Independent initiative

    Zakkingskaart (Dutch for “subsidence map”) is an independent, non-commercial initiative. It is not a website of a municipality or of Rijkswaterstaat and is not made, checked or approved by them. This English version is a translation; in case of differences, the Dutch text prevails.

    What the map shows

    The map shows how fast buildings and the ground in the Netherlands are sinking. It uses three public sources: the benchmarks of Rijkswaterstaat, the Dutch national public works agency (whole country), the settlement bolts of the City of Amsterdam (many more measuring points, Amsterdam only) and satellite measurements of the European Union (whole country). These are measurements, not estimates: where nothing has been measured, the map shows nothing. There is no model that calculates a risk per postcode or per neighbourhood. More explanation: what does the map show?

    Terms

    • mm per year: how many millimetres a point moves down each year. 1 mm per year is 1 centimetre in 10 years.
    • Settlement bolt (meetbout): a small bolt in the façade of a building. The City of Amsterdam regularly measures its height.
    • Benchmark (peilmerk): a small metal bolt in a building or bridge whose height Rijkswaterstaat measures once every 5 to 15 years. The height is measured relative to NAP (Normaal Amsterdams Peil, Amsterdam Ordnance Datum), the standard height reference of the Netherlands.
    • Satellite measurement: satellites use radar to measure the height of ground, streets and buildings together, per block of 100 by 100 metres.
    • Median (or typically): the middle value of a group of measuring points: half sink faster, half slower. That way a single outlier does not determine the picture.
    • Year of construction and addresses: from the BAG, the national register of buildings and addresses. For very old buildings the year of construction is sometimes an estimate. The number of addresses shows, for example, whether a building is an apartment block.
    • Recent years: the rate calculated from the latest measurement and a measurement at least 4 years earlier (at least 8 years for benchmarks).

    Amsterdam: settlement bolts

    In Amsterdam the map uses the city's public settlement bolt data and is updated automatically every week. Settlement bolts are measuring points in façades whose height the city measures periodically. For each bolt the map shows how fast it has sunk in recent years (the recent rate), whether the sinking has clearly slowed down (“stabilised”) and whether there are recent measurements. The city's own map colours by the average rate since the first measurement, so the colours can differ.

    Whole country: Rijkswaterstaat benchmarks

    A benchmark (officially: NAP benchmark, peilmerk) is a small metal bolt in a house, bridge or other structure whose height Rijkswaterstaat measures once every 5 to 15 years. The map shows for each benchmark how fast it has sunk in recent years, with the same colours as the settlement bolts, but as an open circle. Zoomed out you see squares with the median of the benchmarks inside them. There are far fewer benchmarks than settlement bolts: usually a few per neighbourhood. A benchmark moves with the ground of the whole area; it says something about the place, not necessarily about that one building. Benchmarks that have not been measured for more than 20 years, or whose measurements show an unexplained jump, are grey.

    Whole country: subsidence measured from space

    The layer “Subsidence measured from space” comes from Copernicus, the Earth observation programme of the European Union (the service is called the European Ground Motion Service, EGMS). Every few days, satellites use radar to measure the height of ground, streets and buildings. From this, for each block of 100 by 100 metres, it has been calculated how much the ground sank or rose per year over a period of five years. Zoomed out you see the median per block of about 5 or 1 kilometres. You only see blocks where something reflects the radar signal well, such as roofs, roads and bridges; nothing is measured in meadows, fields or on water. Unlike a settlement bolt or benchmark, a block does not measure one building but everything together: a house on piles can stand still while the street in front of it sinks, and vice versa. The colours follow the same classification as the measuring points, but that classification was written for buildings. In its foundation policy, the City of Amsterdam uses satellite data only to see whether a building is sinking more than 3 mm per year. Blocks with an uncertain measurement are grey. The layer is off by default.

    What the colours mean

    The colour depends only on how fast a measuring point has sunk in recent years, in five classes: negligible (less than 0.5 mm per year, or rising), small (0.5 to 2), moderate (2 to 3), large (3 to 4) and very large (4 mm per year or more). These names and limits come from the Dutch national guideline for foundation investigations by the Kennis Centrum Aanpak Funderingsproblematiek (KCAF, Richtlijn Funderingen onder gebouwen, table 3); the City of Amsterdam uses the same classification in its policy rules on existing foundations (2021). A rate exactly on a limit belongs to the higher class. Stabilised: first at least 1.5 and now less than 0.5 mm per year. Grey: no recent or too few measurements. The guideline was written for measurements on buildings; the map also uses it for benchmarks and satellite blocks. The class only describes the measured rate: it says nothing about the condition of the foundation, which can only be established by a foundation investigation.

    No foundation label

    Since 1 April 2026, the valuation of a home in the Netherlands includes a foundation risk in classes A to E, based on data from KCAF. Zakkingskaart does not give such a label and does not estimate any risk. The classes on this map (negligible to very large) only describe how fast a measuring point is sinking; they are not the letters A to E.

    Disclaimer

    This map shows public measurement data from the City of Amsterdam and Rijkswaterstaat in a simpler way and converts it into a rate. In doing so it may differ from how the source presents the data. The map gives no judgement or advice about buildings, the ground or their value, and may be incomplete or out of date. The condition of a building's foundation can only be established by a foundation investigation.     Questions about the data

    This site does not manage the measurement data and cannot change it. Do you have a question about a measurement, or do you think something is wrong? Please contact the source:

    • Settlement bolts (Amsterdam): City of Amsterdam, Basisinformatie, gebruik.basisinformatie@amsterdam.nl (contact address for the dataset Kernregistratie Meetbouten).
    • Benchmarks: Rijkswaterstaat, Servicedesk Data contact form (in Dutch).
    • Subsidence measured from space: Copernicus Land Monitoring Service, Service desk.

    Whatever the source corrects or removes also disappears from this map at the next automatic update. The “Feedback” button is for comments about the website itself.

    Privacy

    This site uses no cookies and does not track visitors. We only count anonymously how often the map is opened per day, from a phone or a computer, via which kind of source (for example a search engine or another website) and how often people search and click. No IP addresses or other characteristics of you are stored. Your map settings are only kept in your own browser. The site is hosted by GitHub, the background map comes from PDOK, an address you search for outside the map data is looked up at the government's PDOK Locatieserver, the year of construction of the building at a clicked spot is requested from PDOK's BAG service, and “View all measurement data” fetches data from the City of Amsterdam or from Rijkswaterstaat; as with any website, those parties see your IP address. Feedback arrives as an email to the maintainer; no name or email address is asked for. The addresses at the measuring points come from the public data of the city and Rijkswaterstaat; questions about them go to the source (see “Questions about the data”).
