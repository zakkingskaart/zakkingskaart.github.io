Explanation: what does Zakkingskaart show?

Zakkingskaart is a visualisation of existing public measurements, classified according to an existing standard (the KCAF guideline, table 3). No estimate per postcode, no judgement or advice and no foundation label. This page explains where the data comes from, what the colours mean and what the map does not tell you.

Measured, not estimated

The map shows measurements, not estimates. A settlement bolt (meetbout) is a small bolt in the façade of one building; the City of Amsterdam regularly measures its height. A benchmark (peilmerk) is a small metal bolt in a building or bridge that Rijkswaterstaat measures relative to NAP, the Dutch height reference. From those measurements the map calculates how fast the point has sunk in recent years. There is also a layer with satellite measurements by the European Union.

There is no model that estimates a risk per postcode, per neighbourhood or per type of house. Where nothing has been measured, the map shows nothing. That way you can see exactly which measurement belongs to which point, with the date of the latest measurement.

What do the colours mean?

The colour depends only on how fast a measuring point has sunk in recent years. The map follows the classification in table 3 of the Richtlijn Funderingen onder gebouwen (guideline on foundations under buildings) of the Kennis Centrum Aanpak Funderingsproblematiek (KCAF). The City of Amsterdam uses the same classification in its policy rules on existing foundations (2021). A rate exactly on a limit belongs to the higher class. 1 mm per year is 1 centimetre in 10 years.

Settlement bolt, benchmark or satellite: what is the difference?

Measuring pointWhat is measuredWhereHow oftenBy whom
Settlement boltthe height of one point in the façade of a buildingAmsterdamperiodically, often once every few yearsCity of Amsterdam
Benchmarkthe height of one point in a building or bridge, relative to NAPwhole countryonce every 5 to 15 yearsRijkswaterstaat
Satellite blockthe height of ground, streets and buildings together, per block of 100 by 100 metreswhole countryevery few days; processed into a rate over five years (2020–2025)Copernicus (European Union)

A settlement bolt says the most about the building it is in. A benchmark moves with the ground of the area; it says something about the place, not necessarily about that one building. A satellite block measures ground, streets and buildings together: a house on piles can stand still while the street in front of it sinks, and vice versa.

And a settlement plate (zakbaak)? That is a plate placed on the original ground before fill is added, with a rod that sticks out above the new ground. Measuring the height of the rod shows how much the subsoil under the fill compacts, for example when a road or a new housing estate is built. Settlement plates usually stay for only a few years and their measurements are rarely public, so they are not on this map.

Is this a foundation label?

No. Since 1 April 2026, foundation risk has been a mandatory part of the valuation report for homes in the Netherlands (the valuers' model report). It includes a risk indication in classes A to E, based on data from KCAF. You can read more about it at KCAF (in Dutch).

Zakkingskaart does not give such a label, does not estimate any risk and says nothing about the condition of a foundation. The classes on this map (negligible to very large) only describe how fast a measuring point has sunk in recent years; they are not the letters A to E. The condition of a building's foundation can only be established by a foundation investigation.

What does the map not tell you?

  • Nothing about the condition of a foundation, the value of a house or what you should do.
  • No forecast: the map shows how fast a point has sunk, not how it will continue.
  • Nothing about buildings without a measuring point. A measuring point says something about that one place; a neighbouring building can sink differently.
  • Measurements can be old, incomplete or wrong. Each point shows the date of its latest measurement.
  • Zakkingskaart is not a government body and not an official source. No rights can be derived from the map.

Something wrong? Report it to the source

Zakkingskaart does not manage the measurement data and does not change it. Questions and reports about a measurement, or about an address at a measuring point, go to the organisation that measures:

  • Settlement bolts (Amsterdam): City of Amsterdam, Basisinformatie, gebruik.basisinformatie@amsterdam.nl (contact address for the public dataset Meetbouten).
  • Benchmarks: Rijkswaterstaat, Servicedesk Data contact form (in Dutch).
  • Satellite measurements: Copernicus Land Monitoring Service, Service desk.

Whatever the source corrects or removes also disappears from this map at the next automatic update. Comments about the website itself can be sent with the “Feedback” button on the map.

Measuring point at your address not on this site?

Do you live at an address with a measuring point, or own it, and do you not want it on this site? Enter the address as it appears on the map. The measuring point disappears automatically at the next update (usually within a week), also from the pages per street and neighbourhood. The street page then only says how many measuring points are not shown on request, without addresses. We do not ask for a name or email address. You receive a personal code with which you can have it shown again later. The City of Amsterdam and Rijkswaterstaat continue to publish their data themselves; if something is wrong with a measurement, only the source can correct it (see above).


Hidden earlier and want it shown again?

Enter the same address and the code you received when you made the request. The measuring point returns at the next update. Without that code nobody can undo a request.





How up to date is the map?

The map is updated automatically with the latest public data; most recently on 2026-11-27. Settlement bolts and benchmarks are not measured every year: each point shows when it was last measured. Points without a recent measurement are grey. The satellite data covers 2020–2025; the European Union publishes a new version once a year.

Who makes the map?

Zakkingskaart is an independent initiative, separate from the City of Amsterdam, Rijkswaterstaat, KCAF and Copernicus. The map is free, uses no cookies and does not track visitors.
